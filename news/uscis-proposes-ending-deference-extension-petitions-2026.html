Proposal: USCIS wants to stop deferring to prior approvals when extending H-1B, L-1 and other work visas

October 21, 2026 · On 14 October 2026 USCIS published a proposed rule to remove 8 CFR 214.1(c)(5), the regulation that tells officers to defer to an earlier approval when deciding an extension petition. It is only a proposal: comments are open until 13 November 2026, and nothing changes until a final rule is published.

Status: proposal, not law. On October 14, 2026 the Department of Homeland Security published a notice of proposed rulemaking (NPRM) in the Federal Register. It would delete 8 CFR 214.1(c)(5), the regulation that generally requires USCIS officers to defer to a prior approval involving the same employer, the same worker and the same facts when they decide a request to extend a petition. Written comments are accepted until November 13, 2026 (docket USCIS-2026-0529 on regulations.gov). Until a final rule is published, the current deference rule stays in force.

Background: deference started as a USCIS memo in April 2004, was rescinded in October 2017, returned to the USCIS Policy Manual in April 2021 and became a regulation on December 18, 2024 as part of the H-1B modernization rule. Under the current rule an officer may refuse deference only for a material error, a material change in circumstances or new material information, and must explain why.

What would change: every extension filed on Form I-129 would be reviewed from scratch, as if it were a first petition. This covers H-1B, H-1B1, L-1, O-1, E-1, E-2, E-3, TN, P, R-1 and other employment categories. DHS states the proposal does not forbid officers from considering a prior approval; it removes the duty to start from deference. DHS expects more Requests for Evidence (RFEs) and counts the extra paperwork time as the main cost of the rule. Across fiscal years 2014 to 2025 about 30 percent of all I-129 petitions were extension cases eligible for deference.

Why it matters: an extension that used to be routine could get an RFE on the job duties, the degree, the wage level or the employer's business, and a denial can mean a gap in status. To estimate how many extra RFEs to expect, DHS used the 2017 to 2021 period, when deference was not in effect.

What to do now: if your H-1B or L-1 extension is due in 2027, keep the same quality of evidence as in the first petition: a current job description, pay records, employer documents, licences and a degree evaluation.
